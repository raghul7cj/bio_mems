1The whole project in one picture

A paper test strip has no pump. Liquid is pulled along by capillarity, the same effect that makes a paper towel soak up water. We place four electrodes along the strip. Each one reports the moment the wet front reaches it. Physics predicts those moments for a healthy strip, so when the measured times disagree, the pattern of disagreement tells us what went wrong and roughly where.

The project has three layers, and this guide follows them:

  1. Physics: how fast the front moves, and what each fault does to that motion.
  2. Decision rules: how to turn four arrival times into a verdict, using only what the physics allows us to know.
  3. Hardware: a small circuit that applies those rules in real time, and the checks that prove it is correct.

2Surface tension and wetting

Molecules inside a liquid are pulled equally in all directions by their neighbours. Molecules at the surface have neighbours on one side only, so they are pulled inward. The surface therefore behaves like a stretched skin, and making more of it costs energy. That energy per unit area is the surface tension γ (water: 0.072 N/m, the same as 0.072 J/m²).

When the liquid touches a solid, the edge meets it at a contact angle θ. Water on clean cellulose has θ ≈ 0°: it spreads and wets the surface. A large θ (above 90°) means the liquid beads up, like water on wax. That is exactly why printed wax lines act as walls in paper devices.

In a narrow tube a wetting liquid forms a curved surface, the meniscus. A curved surface carries a pressure jump across it, the Laplace pressure:

ΔP = 2 γ cos θ / rThe narrower the tube (small r) and the better the wetting (θ near 0), the stronger the pull.

Example. Water in a 10 µm pore: ΔP = 2 × 0.072 / 10⁻⁵ ≈ 14,000 Pa, about 14 % of atmospheric pressure. For the real nitrocellulose membrane, the paper by Kasetsirikul et al. estimates an effective capillary pressure of about 2,300 Pa. This pressure is the "battery" that drives all flow in the strip.

Viscous flow and resistance

Liquid also resists being pushed. Viscosity μ measures that resistance: water is 0.89 mPa·s, blood plasma about 1.3 and whole blood about 3.5. In a thin tube, flow is slow and smooth (laminar), and the average speed follows the Hagen–Poiseuille law:

v̄ = r² ΔP / (8 μ L)L is the length of liquid in the tube. A longer liquid column means more friction, so slower flow.

The electrical analogy is the most useful way to think about it: pressure is voltage, flow rate is current, and the tube is a resistor whose resistance grows in proportion to its length. As the liquid fills more of the tube, the resistor gets longer and the current falls. That is the whole reason a wicking front slows down.

Washburn's law, derived

Combine the two ideas: the capillary pull from §2 drives the viscous flow from §3. Put ΔP = 2γ cosθ / r into Poiseuille, and the front speed is v̄ = dL/dt:

dL/dt = r² / (8 μ L) · 2 γ cos θ / r = r γ cos θ / (4 μ L)

Multiply both sides by L. The right side no longer depends on L or t:

L · dL/dt = r γ cos θ / (4 μ) → d(L²)/dt = r γ cos θ / (2 μ)

A quantity that grows at a constant rate is just that rate times time:

L² = K t, K = γ r cos θ / (2 μ)Lucas (1918), Washburn (1921). K has units of mm²/s.
What this means. Distance grows like the square root of time. To wick twice as far takes four times as long. With our measured K = 9.42 mm²/s, reaching 20 mm takes 400 / 9.42 = 42.5 s, and reaching 40 mm takes 1600 / 9.42 = 170 s. The speed is dL/dt = K / (2L): 2.4 mm/s near the inlet, 0.12 mm/s at 40 mm.

Paper is not a bundle of tubes

Real paper is a tangled mat of fibres. Its pores have many sizes, and the path through them is winding. The proper description is Darcy's law for porous media: flow = (permeability × area / viscosity) × pressure gradient. Permeability k is a property of the paper, a measure of how easily liquid passes through it.

Darcy still gives L² = K t, but K now bundles the capillary pressure, the permeability, the porosity and the viscosity together. We cannot predict K from a single "pore radius". Two ways of computing an effective radius from our measured numbers give 0.23 µm and 61 µm, which disagree by a factor of 270. So we measure K instead, by fitting published wicking data.

Calibration in one lineWe read 33 points off a published curve (nitrocellulose FF170HP with water) and fitted L² = K t: K = 9.42 mm²/s. The paper's own fit gives 9.73, and the manufacturer's "170 s per 4 cm" rating gives 9.41. The experiment was done vertically, and correcting for gravity would give about 9 % more, so we carry that as an uncertainty.

The generator model used to create the test data uses Darcy flow through a series of segments. Each little piece of wetted paper is a resistor, with resistance proportional to its length divided by its permeability and width. The front speed is the capillary pressure divided by the total resistance behind it. With uniform paper this reduces exactly to Washburn.

6Dimensionless numbers: which forces can we ignore?

A dimensionless number is the ratio of two effects. If it is much smaller than 1, the effect on top can be ignored. This is how physicists justify a simple model.

7Faults as physics

Every fault the detector looks for is a change to one term in the physics.

Too little samplethe source runs drythe front creeps a little further, then stops
Sensor fault: missed, spurious, stucknothing physical; the reading is wrongtimings that physics cannot produce

Measuring a restriction. A stretch of paper of width w with only a fraction k of normal permeability has the resistance of a longer piece of normal paper. The extra length is

ΔL_eq = (1/k − 1) · wExample: 4 mm of paper at 1/6 permeability behaves like (6 − 1) × 4 = 20 mm of extra paper.

Running out of sample. The paper holds 10.1 µL per cm² (measured by weighing). A 4 mm wide strip needs 0.404 µL per mm, so 16 µL fills 40 mm. With less sample the front stops short, and the electrodes past that point never see liquid.

8What the electrodes see

Each electrode reports one number, the time the front reached it. We compare consecutive differences, the time to cross each segment, with the Washburn prediction for water:

Expected segment time: E_k = (L_k² − L_{k−1}²) / K
Segment ratio: ρ_k = measured Δt_k / E_kρ = 1: as fast as water. ρ = 2: twice as slow.

Why the odd electrode positions? Washburn says time depends on L². Putting the electrodes where L² steps evenly (L² = 400, 800, 1200, 1600 mm², so L = 20, 28.3, 34.6, 40 mm) makes every segment take the same 42.5 s for water. Each segment then gets equal timing resolution.

9What can and cannot be told apart

This is the scientific core of the project. Ask what the measurement could reveal before asking how well an algorithm does.

Which fluid property changed: never

The arrival times depend on γ, θ and μ only through K ∝ γ cosθ / μ. Doubling the viscosity, halving the surface tension, or raising the contact angle from 0° to 60° (cos 60° = 0.5) all halve K. In the project's simulation they give identical arrival times, to the millisecond. No algorithm, however clever, can separate them with arrival times alone. The detector can say "the fluid is slower than water", not "the viscosity is high".

A restriction: only if it is big enough

For segments fully downstream of a restriction of size ΔL, the segment ratio rises to about

ρ_k ≈ 1 + 2 ΔL / (L_k + L_{k−1})

The same ΔL matters less further down the strip, because more resistance has already built up there. In the simulation a restriction is reliably seen once ΔL_eq exceeds about 10 mm in segment 1, 13 mm in segment 2 and 15 mm in segment 3. A "mild" 4 mm restriction changes the timing by under 10 %, which is smaller than the normal strip-to-strip spread, so it hides.

A restriction before the first electrode: partly

For a restriction right at the start of the strip, the extra resistance is present from the beginning, so t ∝ L² + 2ΔL·L. The extra term grows only like L, while the normal term grows like L², so the ratio falls along the strip (for ΔL = 20 mm: about 3.0, 1.8, 1.6, 1.5). A strong one produces this non-Washburn pattern, and the detector flags it as an ANOMALY. A weak one leaves the ratios almost flat, which is indistinguishable from a slower fluid.

One sentence for the TA. Arrival times measure K and how K varies along the strip. Anything that changes K in the same way everywhere looks the same, so we can classify the kind of fault and its location, but not the molecular cause.

11The detector's rules, explained

  1. Was anything already wet? An electrode reading "wet" before the sample is applied is a broken sensor: SENSOR.
  2. Learn this sample's speed from the first segment. r̂ = Δt₀ / E₀. Blood is about 3.9× slower than water, and that is fine. The detector adapts instead of raising an alarm. This is the "online estimate of K".
  3. Wait for each next electrode, with a window that scales. Segment k may take up to 3 × r̂ × E_k. Past that, the front has stopped: STALL, at that segment. A fixed timeout uses one window for every sample, so it cannot say where the front stopped, and it misjudges fast or slow fluids.
  4. Reject impossible timings. Electrodes firing out of order or at the same instant, or a first segment more than twice as fast as water (no real sample is), mean SENSOR.
  5. Compare every segment with the first. The first segment that is more than 19.5 % slower than r̂ predicts is a RESTRICTION, located there. Much faster is an ANOMALY, the signature of an upstream restriction.
  6. If all segments agree, compare the total time with water's: within ±14.8 % is NOMINAL; slower or faster is UNIFORM_SLOW or UNIFORM_FAST, a fluid change.

Why cross-multiplication?

Rule 5 asks whether (Δt_k / E_k) / (Δt₀ / E₀) > 1.195. Division is expensive in hardware and introduces rounding. Multiply out instead: Δt_k · E₀ · 256 > Δt₀ · E_k · 306, since 306/256 = 1.195. Both sides are whole numbers, so the hardware and the Python reference model get exactly the same answer.

Worked exampleRestriction case from §8, in milliseconds: Δt₀ = 42463, Δt₂ = 55960, and every E = 42463. The E's cancel, so the test is 55960 × 256 = 14,325,760 against 42463 × 306 = 12,993,678. The left side is bigger, so the verdict is RESTRICTION in segment 2.

12How performance is measured

  • Detection rate: of the strips that really had a fault, the fraction flagged. Ours: 88.9 %; a fixed timeout: 46.8 %.
  • False-alarm rate: of the healthy strips, the fraction wrongly flagged. Any method can catch every fault by flagging everything, so detection only means something at a fixed false-alarm rate. All methods were tuned to about 5–7 %.
  • Calibration vs test split: thresholds were chosen on one half of the simulated strips and scored on the other half. Tuning and scoring on the same data flatters the method, the same way seeing the exam paper in advance would.
  • Confusion matrix: rows are what really happened, columns are what the detector said. The diagonal is right; anything off it is a specific kind of mistake.
  • Jitter σ: the random error in each arrival time. Real electrodes show about 1.4 s, worked out from the published data.
  • IQR, the interquartile range: the middle 50 % of a set of values. The paper reports "30 s (29–39)": half the samples took between 29 and 39 s.

13From physics to hardware

Physics or ruleHardware element
Arrival time at an electrodeInput-capture unit: stores the 1 ms counter value when the comparator line goes high
Expected segment times E_k and tolerancesConstants in a small ROM, with constant × constant products folded at build time
Ratio tests (rules 3, 5, 6)One shared 24 × 36-bit multiplier and 64-bit comparators; no divider
Rule sequenceA 16-state finite-state machine
Electrical noise on the electrode line2-flip-flop synchronizer (against metastability), then a glitch filter: the line must stay high for 10 µs
Verdict3-bit class and 3-bit location, with a one-cycle valid pulse

Why decide from timestamps only? The timestamp is taken at the start of the high pulse, so filtering adds no timing error. The state machine also acts only on stored timestamps, never on "what cycle is it now". Its answer is therefore the same at any clock speed. That let the 9,900 test cases be simulated with one tick per clock (about 10⁵× faster than real time), and every case matched the Python model exactly.

How correctness was established, in four layers: an independent reference model written from the spec, not from the RTL; exact agreement on all 9,900 cases; assertions that fail if the circuit ever gives a result before the evidence exists; and coverage, which shows that every state, transition and outcome was actually exercised. On top of that, six deliberately broken copies of the circuit were all caught by the assertions.

14The real-data check

Bezinge et al. (2024) printed graphene electrodes inside a paper test and sent 100 mV pulses every 0.1 s. When liquid flows over an electrode, its capacitive discharge current changes, so a jump in the current difference marks flow. We re-implemented their algorithm in Python, ran it on their published data, and reproduced their flow times: 29.2, 19.5 and 40.6 s, all inside the ranges reported in the paper. Their comparison with visual timing also tells us how precise real electrodes are, about 1.4 s per event, and the simulations used that range. Their device has a single electrode position, so it checks timing, not the four-electrode classification.

15Questions the TA may ask

Why does distance grow like √t and not like t?

Because the resistance grows with the wetted length while the driving capillary pressure stays fixed. Speed is pressure divided by resistance, which is proportional to 1/L, so L·dL/dt is constant and L² = Kt.

Why not compute K from the pore size?

Paper has no single pore size, and its paths are tortuous. Two estimates of an effective radius from our own measured numbers disagree by 270×. Fitting K to measured wicking captures all of that in one number, and it agreed with two independent checks to within 3 %.

Why can you ignore gravity?

The Bond number is at most 5×10⁻⁴ inside pores, and the strip is horizontal. Gravity only mattered in the vertical calibration experiment, which is why we carry a 9 % systematic uncertainty on K.

Can your device tell high viscosity from low surface tension?

No, and no device measuring arrival times can. Both enter only through K ∝ γ cosθ / μ. We showed three different changes giving identical timings to the millisecond. We classify "the fluid is slower than water", not its cause.

Why four electrodes, and why at those positions?

One electrode gives a single time, which cannot say where a problem is. Several give a pattern. Spacing them evenly in L² makes each segment take the same expected time (42.5 s), so every segment gets equal timing resolution.

What is your main improvement over a fixed timeout?

About twice the detection rate at the same false-alarm rate (88.9 % against 46.8 %), plus a cause and a location. The extra electrodes give the detection. The physics-based rules give the classification, and they adapt to the sample's own speed.

Why did mild restrictions go undetected?

A 4 mm-equivalent restriction changes a segment's timing by under 10 %, which is less than the normal strip-to-strip spread plus timing noise. We computed the threshold: about 10–15 mm, depending on position.

How do you know the hardware is right?

An independent Python model written from the same spec gives the expected answer for 9,900 cases; the RTL matched all of them. Assertions check rules such as "no result before its evidence"; coverage shows every state and outcome was exercised; and six deliberately broken designs were all caught.

What is the weakest part of the project?

The four-electrode results come from a physics simulation, not from a measured strip. The real data we checked against has only one electrode. The next step would be a printed strip with four electrodes.

16Check yourself

1. A strip wicks 20 mm in 42.5 s. How long to 30 mm?

t ∝ L²: 42.5 × (30/20)² = 95.6 s.

2. The sample is 3× more viscous than water. What do all four ratios become?

About 3, 3, 3, 3. K drops by 3×, so every segment takes 3× longer. The pattern is flat, so the verdict is UNIFORM_SLOW.

3. The ratios read 1.0, 1.0, 1.5, 1.4. What happened, and where?

A restriction in segment 2: normal before it, slow from it onward. Segment 3 stays slow because the extra resistance stays in the circuit.

4. Ratios 2.2, 1.8, 1.6, 1.5, falling. What does that suggest?

A restriction before the first electrode. A constant extra resistance adds a term ∝ L that matters less as L grows. The detector reports ANOMALY.

5. Electrodes 0 and 1 fire, then nothing. Is it a stall or a broken electrode 2?

If electrode 3 also never fires, the detector reports STALL in segment 2. If electrode 3 fires later, then electrode 2 missed its arrival: SENSOR. A missed last electrode cannot be told apart from a stall in the last segment.

6. Why is the first-segment floor set at half the water time?

No plausible sample wicks twice as fast as water (water at 37 °C is only 1.29× faster). A faster first segment means a false early trigger, so SENSOR.
